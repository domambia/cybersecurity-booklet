# Chapter 35 · Your First 90 Days

> **Why this chapter matters:** Getting hired is the start line, not the finish. Your first 90 days set your reputation and trajectory in ways that are hard to change later. Security is a high-trust, high-stakes field where a new person can either quickly become someone the team relies on or someone they work around. The good news: succeeding early is mostly about attitude, learning approach, and a few avoidable mistakes — not raw brilliance. This chapter shows you how to land well, add value fast, and start compounding into a real career.

> **By the end of this chapter you will:** know how to onboard effectively, learn your specific environment fast, add value early without overstepping, avoid the classic rookie mistakes, and build the relationships and habits that turn a first job into a career.

---

## 35.1 The mindset: humble, curious, reliable

Three traits define successful first-90-days people, and none require genius:
- **Humble** — you know a lot of *general* security (this booklet), but you know almost nothing about *this specific organization* — its systems, tools, politics, history, and "why we do it this way." Assume there are good reasons for things you don't understand yet. The new hire who declares "you're all doing it wrong" in week two torches their credibility; the one who asks "help me understand why we do X" builds it.
- **Curious** — ask questions (thoughtfully — see below), read the documentation, understand the environment. Genuine curiosity is the fastest path to competence and signals the learning mindset the field runs on.
- **Reliable** — do what you say, follow through, admit mistakes fast, don't cut corners. In a trust-based field (Chapter 3), reliability *is* your reputation. Being dependable matters more early than being brilliant.

> **The core reframe:** your job in the first 90 days is primarily to **learn and become trusted**, not to prove you're the smartest person in the room. Value comes from becoming genuinely useful in *this* context, which takes listening first.

---

## 35.2 Learn your specific environment fast

Your general knowledge is the foundation; now you map it onto *this* organization. Prioritize learning:
- **The environment** — what systems, networks, and assets exist? What's critical (the crown jewels)? What's the architecture? (Your threat-modeling and recon instincts — Chapters 10, 14 — apply internally now.)
- **The tools** — the specific SIEM, EDR, ticketing, cloud platform, and internal tooling *this* team uses. You know the *concepts* (Parts 4–5); now learn *their* implementation. Concepts transfer; specifics are learned on the job.
- **The processes** — how does this team triage, escalate, respond, and communicate? What are the playbooks (Chapter 22)? What's the on-call rhythm? Follow the established process before suggesting changes to it.
- **The people** — who owns what? Who do you go to for what? Who are the informal experts? Relationships determine how effectively you can do anything.
- **The "why"** — the history and constraints behind current decisions. Understanding *why* things are the way they are prevents you from naively "fixing" things that are the way they are for good reasons (or spotting the ones that genuinely need fixing, credibly).

**How to learn it fast:** read all the documentation, shadow experienced colleagues, take detailed notes (your Chapter 1 system, applied to the job), ask to be walked through real cases, and volunteer for varied work to see more of the environment.

---

## 35.3 Asking questions well

You must ask questions — but *how* you ask shapes your reputation:
- **Do your homework first.** Check the docs, search internal wikis, try to find the answer before asking. "I looked at X and Y and I'm stuck on Z" respects people's time and shows initiative; "how do I do everything?" doesn't.
- **Batch and time them** — don't interrupt constantly; save non-urgent questions for appropriate moments.
- **Ask the "why," not just the "how."** Understanding reasoning builds real competence and shows you're thinking, not just executing.
- **Take notes so you don't ask the same thing twice** — asking once is learning; asking repeatedly signals you're not retaining.
- **It's fine to not know things** — you're new. Bluffing is the danger, not asking. (Confident wrongness gets people breached — Chapter 3/19; this applies internally too.)

> A useful heuristic: **struggle briefly, then ask.** Spend a reasonable, bounded effort trying yourself (that's where learning happens), but don't burn a full day stuck on something a colleague could unblock in two minutes. Judgment about *when* to ask is itself a skill.

## 35.4 Adding value early (without overstepping)

You want to contribute, but calibrated to your standing:
- **Start with the work you're given, done well.** Reliable execution of your actual assignments builds the credibility you'll need for bigger things. Nail the basics first.
- **Take the tasks others avoid** — documentation, tuning noisy alerts (Chapter 21), writing/updating playbooks, cleaning up backlogs. These are genuinely valuable, teach you the environment deeply, and build goodwill. Your CS-grad ability to *automate* tedious things (Chapter 8) is an early, welcome superpower — automating a manual chore endears you fast.
- **Bring your fresh perspective *carefully*.** New eyes do spot real problems. Raise them as *questions and observations*, not criticisms: "I noticed X; is there a reason we do it this way, or is that something worth looking at?" This respects the team while still contributing your value.
- **Fresh CS/AI skills are an asset** — if you can script a solution, build a small tool, or bring AI-security literacy (Chapter 29) the team lacks, that's real value — offered helpfully, not to show off.
- **Don't try to boil the ocean.** Small, reliable wins compound. Overpromising and underdelivering early is worse than modest, consistent delivery.

## 35.5 The classic rookie mistakes to avoid

- **Overconfidence / criticizing before understanding.** The fastest way to lose credibility. Understand *why* before proposing change.
- **Bluffing instead of admitting you don't know.** Dangerous in a trust-based, high-stakes field. "I'll find out" is respected; confident wrongness is not.
- **Not asking for help and staying stuck.** Burning days on something a colleague could unblock quickly wastes everyone's time.
- **Being careless with access.** You now have privileged access to real systems and real data (Chapter 3). Follow procedures exactly, don't poke around out of curiosity where you're not authorized, and take data handling seriously. A new hire who mishandles access loses trust instantly — and possibly the job.
- **Ignoring the human/soft side.** Relationships and communication determine your effectiveness more than raw technical skill (Chapter 19). Invest in them.
- **Not documenting your own work.** Keep notes and document what you do — for yourself, for handoffs, and because it's a professional norm (and audit reality — Chapter 30).
- **Neglecting continued learning.** The field moves fast; the job doesn't stop your learning (Chapter 36). Keep the Chapter 1 habits.

---

## 35.6 Building relationships and your reputation

- **Get to know your team** — not just what they do, but as people. Trust is built through everyday interactions.
- **Find mentors** — people (in and beyond your team) who'll guide you. Most experienced practitioners will help someone who shows genuine effort and humility. Ask for guidance; it's a strength, not a weakness.
- **Communicate proactively** — update people on your progress, flag blockers early, don't go silent. Especially: **escalate real issues promptly** rather than hiding uncertainty (Chapter 22's "know when to escalate," applied daily).
- **Be someone people want to work with** — helpful, reliable, positive, honest. In the long run, reputation and relationships open more doors than any single skill. The security community is small and long-memoried; your reputation follows you across jobs.
- **Keep your public learning going** (Chapter 32) — but be careful never to share anything confidential about your employer (Chapter 3). Write about general concepts, not internal specifics.

> **The compounding effect:** the trust and reputation you build in the first 90 days determine what you're *allowed* to do next — the interesting projects, the growth opportunities, the autonomy. Land well, and the career compounds from there. This is why the "boring" virtues — humility, reliability, curiosity, integrity — matter more early than any flashy skill.

## Labs

> **Lab 35.1 — Make a 90-day learning plan.** Before/at the start of a role, draft a plan: what to learn about the environment, tools, processes, and people in weeks 1–4, 5–8, 9–12. Include "meet these people," "read these docs," "shadow these tasks." Adapt on the job. (You can draft a *generic* version now as a rehearsal.)

> **Lab 35.2 — Question-asking practice.** Write out how you'd ask three "new hire" questions using the good pattern from §35.3 ("I looked at X and Y, I'm stuck on Z, is it A or B?"). Notice how different they are from lazy questions. This habit builds your reputation.

> **Lab 35.3 — Identify your early-value plays.** List three types of "tasks others avoid" (documentation, alert tuning, automation of a manual chore) you could volunteer for early, and how your CS/scripting skills would make them wins. Have these ready for day one.

> **Lab 35.4 — Draft your "how I raise concerns" script.** Write the phrasing you'd use to surface a potential problem as a curious question rather than a criticism. Rehearse it. This one skill prevents the #1 rookie mistake.

> **Lab 35.5 — Set up your on-the-job learning system.** Extend your Chapter 1 note system with sections for: environment map, tools/how-tos, people/who-owns-what, and open questions. A good system makes you competent fast and prevents re-asking.

## References and further reading

- **Michael Watkins — *The First 90 Days*.** The classic on starting any new role well (onboarding, early wins, building credibility). Directly applicable; read it before you start.
- **"The Unwritten Laws of Engineering" — W.J. King** — timeless, short guidance on professional conduct and reputation for technical people.
- **Camille Fournier — *The Manager's Path* (early chapters)** — understanding how technical careers and organizations work, from the individual-contributor view up.
- **Your team's internal documentation, runbooks, and playbooks** — the most important reading in your first weeks. Read them all.
- **Simply Cyber, mentorship communities (e.g., security Discords, local chapters)** — for finding mentors and peer support as you start.
- **Your company's security policies and code of conduct** (Chapters 3, 30) — read and internalize; they govern your privileged access.
